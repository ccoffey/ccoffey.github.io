Claw Machine

First-Principles Arcade Build Inspired by Tokyo's GiGO: From Breadboard to Custom KiCad PCB

ESP32 KiCad PCB Design 3D Printing Gear Design
Build Demonstration

The Build Story

This build was sparked during a family vacation to Tokyo, where my kids became completely obsessed by the hundreds of claw machines towering through the GiGO building in Akihabara. Standing there watching them play, I distinctly remember saying to myself: "When I get home, I'm going to build one of these from first principles." This was my very first electronics project. Armed with a crappy old soldering iron dug out of my attic, I bought a stepper motor and an ESP32-WROOM board, got the shaft to spin, and the obsession took off.

I started prototyping a linear rail: my initial lead-screw drive was far too slow for an arcade game, so I pivoted to belt drives, 3D printing custom carriages and rail mounts until the carriage zipped left and right at arcade speed. Adding a perpendicular rail unlocked full 2D X/Y motion, and wiring up an arcade joystick to control the carriage (which eventually inspired my 2-player arcade cabinet!) brought it to life. Solving the Z-axis was the hardest mechanical challenge: I needed holding torque so the claw wouldn't drop under gravity when motors were unpowered. After discovering worm-and-wheel gears, I fell in love with self-locking holding torque, 3D printing 20 iterations of worms and gears—dialing in thread starts, pitch, and gear ratios—until the drop was snappy and reliable.

The claw itself is a solenoid-driven mechanism sourced from AliExpress: energizing pulls the claws closed; de-energizing releases. Unlike commercial arcade machines that use PWM pulse tricks to deliberately weaken claw strength and cheat players, my machine is 100% fair every single time—the players are my kids, and the goal is pure fun. The project started as a rat's nest of breadboards and jumper wires. Juggling four separate voltage rails (3.3V, 5V, 12V, and 24V), I accidentally cross-shorted rails and built a small graveyard of blown ESP32s. To stop frying chips, I taught myself PCB layout; a week later I was completely hooked on KiCad and PCBWay. It took just two board revisions to produce a rock-solid custom PCB integrating motor drivers, DFPlayer Mini sound effects, WS2812B lighting, an arcade coin acceptor, and an optical laser break-beam sensor to detect winning prize drops.

Once everything worked on my workbench, I framed the full-scale cabinet out of 2×4 timber and MDF, installed custom-cut acrylic viewing panes on three sides, and hinged the side door for easy prize reloading. The cabinet is mechanically complete and in daily use—it's still in its raw timber state, with priming, painting, and artwork waiting for a future weekend.

Next Steps & Planned Upgrades

  • Paint & Graphics: Sand, prime, and apply custom arcade-style cabinet graphics and marquee artwork to finish the raw timber.
  • Slack-line claw drop sensor: Redesign the claw drop mechanism so that it detects when the claw goes slack instead of the drop being a fixed amount of time (which doesn't work well when prizes are stacked high or non-uniformly).
  • Dual optical break-beam chute: Add a second beam sensor to the exit chute so that we don't miss small prize wins.
