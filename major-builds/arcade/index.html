Arcade Cabinet

A two-player cabinet combining precision controls, custom fabrication, 3D-printed surfacing and original-console input.

Parametric Layout Woodworking 3D Printing Linux Input RetroPie
Build Demonstration

The Build Story

This was fundamentally a woodworking, 3D printing, and automotive finishing project. I framed the internal skeleton from 2×2 Planed White Deal timber and skinned it with MDF. To create the exterior contours, I skinned the cabinet with 3D-printed PLA panels—piecing them together like a giant jigsaw puzzle because my Prusa build plate is only 200×200 mm. To eliminate the seams, I filled the joints with Isopon P38 body filler (typically used in car repair—a first for me), followed by endless rounds of sanding, sprayed automotive filler primer (learning spray-can finishing technique on the fly), and finished with leftover paint from my attic that had been used for painting interior doors in my house.

The electronics were intentionally kept straightforward: a Raspberry Pi 5 running RetroPie with an external hard drive and an Amazon sound system. To make the cabinet truly special, I bought and disassembled USB adapters for original Nintendo 64 and GameCube controllers, integrating the ports directly into the cabinet so I could play the games I loved as a kid together with my own children using my original childhood controllers.

One of the hardest parts of the project was getting the joystick positions and button ergonomics correct, requiring multiple drilling templates and extensive trial-and-error. The control panel is wrapped in a custom wide-format vinyl graphic printed by Gorilla Graphics (gorillagraphics.ie).

Engineering at a glance

Working cabinet · GameCube ports and final detailing remaining

Precision layout

A parametric 796 × 248 mm control panel

A configuration-driven Python tool places both Japanese-style control clusters in absolute millimetre coordinates, then generates drill overlays and tiled 1:1 test prints with a physical calibration bar.

Fabrication

Timber structure, MDF body and a printed outer skin

The cabinet combines a 2×2 timber frame with MDF panels and many 200×200 mm PLA sections. Automotive body filler, repeated sanding and high-build primer turn the printed jigsaw into a continuous surface.

Controls integration

Four controllers made predictable on Linux

Two DragonRise arcade encoders and a dual-port original N64 adapter are mapped deliberately across Linux joystick devices, RetroArch and Mupen64Plus, with a live diagnostic tool for tracing inputs layer by layer.

Production tooling

Artwork and drilling share one source of geometry

The same panel definition produces clean print-shop artwork, proof images and drilling templates, reducing the chance that a visually correct graphic and the physical controls drift out of alignment.
